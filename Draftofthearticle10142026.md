Title: Root water uptake beyond root length density: Insights from SWaP experiments and process-based modelling.
Abstract
A major challenge in the fields of plant and soil sciences is understanding how soil, root system, and plant hydraulics interact to control root water uptake (RWU). This study combines high-resolution Soil Water Profiler (SWaP) measurements, MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) of root systems, and a one-dimensional (1D) soil–plant hydraulic model to quantify the spatial and temporal patterns of root water uptake (RWU) in Vicia faba grown in two substrates: sandy soil and sandy loam. SWaP provided high-resolution vertical soil water content (θ) profiles under both controlled environmental conditions and alternating light intensities, allowed the derivation of water depletion rates and plant-driven uptake (Ûp). MRI supplied detailed root length density (RLD) profiles that were used to characterize roots behavior and parameterize root system hydraulics. We used a macroscopic, one-dimensional (1D), Richards-based model that incorporates soil, rhizosphere, and root hydraulic compartments to simulate soil water dynamics and RWU. Introduction
Soil texture plays a key role in the dynamics of plant-soil interactions, shaping how water moves through the soil and its availability to plants. Different textures affect soil hydraulic properties, including hydraulic conductivity and soil-water diffusivity, which are essential for understanding water retention and movement in the soil profile (Gardner, 1960).  Studies on kaolinitic clay soils have shown that even small differences within a particular soil type can have a significant impact on hydraulic properties. Variations in microstructure, including differences in organic matter and clay composition, are critical in influencing soil hydraulic conductivity and water-holding capacity. This variability in soil texture introduces substantial uncertainty of soil hydraulic model parameterization, which consequently influences the prediction of water balance components (De Jong van Lier and Pinheiro, 2021). In his study, Cai et al. (2021) showed that soil texture significantly affects root water uptake by influencing the water potential at the soil-root interface. In sandy soils, hydraulic conductivity decreases more rapidly with decreasing soil water content than in loamy soils. This steeper decrease in hydraulic conductivity in sandy soils leads to a more pronounced decrease in soil-root interface water potential, which in turn triggers earlier stomatal closure and a more rapid decrease in transpiration rates. As a result, plants in sandy soils experience a greater reduction in root water uptake during drought, highlighting the central role of soil texture in influencing plant responses to water stress. Wankmüller et al (2024) further elucidated that, when the soil dries and below a critical soil water potential threshold (ψcrit), stomatal closure is triggered by a decrease in leaf water potential (ψleaf) and soil–plant hydraulic conductance, to downregulate 
transpiration. This critical threshold, and how quickly it is reached, is fundamentally influenced by a complex interplay of atmospheric conditions, plant characteristics, and soil properties.
Root water uptake (RWU) represents the key process linking soil water availability to plant transpiration and therefore plays a central role in soil–plant–atmosphere interactions. Quantifying the spatial distribution of RWU within the soil profile is essential for understanding soil drying dynamics, plant water use, and plant responses to drought. However, direct measurements of RWU remain challenging, particularly when attempting to resolve water uptake at different soil depths. Consequently, many studies estimate RWU indirectly from soil water depletion measurements or approximate its spatial distribution based on the vertical distribution of roots.
