# When Is Plant-Driven Uptake (Ûp) a Reliable Proxy for Root Density (SUF)?

### A Depth- and Time-Resolved Validation of the SWaP Assumption Using a Mechanistically Simulated Root–Soil System

---

## Abstract

The Soil Water Profiler (SWaP) method (van Dusschoten et al., 2020) estimates the plant-driven component of root water uptake, Ûp(z), from soil water content time series alone, by regressing the local depletion rate against total plant transpiration within a short time window under the assumption that soil water redistribution is approximately linear in time within that window. This assumption has previously been justified through diffusion-timescale arguments and in-silico tests using a passive, non-compensating synthetic sink. Here we test it under more demanding conditions, by applying the SWaP regression to soil water content generated by a fully mechanistic Couvreur hydraulic-architecture model, in which genuine compensatory root water uptake is physically active. Because the true sink term is known exactly in this simulated system, we are able to validate Ûp(z) against ground truth rather than assume its accuracy. We find that the reliability of Ûp(z) as a proxy for the true Standard Uptake Fraction, SUF(z), depends jointly on soil depth and on the phase of the transpiration cycle. Reliability is highest near the surface, where root density and hence the passive uptake baseline are large, and at the start and end of each transpiration pulse, when the sink has reached a quasi-steady state. Reliability collapses at intermediate depths and mid-cycle, where compensatory uptake becomes comparable in magnitude to passive uptake and evolves on a timescale similar to the imposed transpiration forcing itself, violating the linear-in-time assumption underlying the SWaP regression. We propose a structural explanation for this depth-dependence, rooted in the proportionality between passive uptake and root density, and a three-term diagnostic that separates true compensation from soil-capillary estimation artefacts.

---

## 1. Introduction

Non-invasive estimation of the vertical distribution of root water uptake is central to linking plant water use to root system function under field conditions. The SWaP method achieves this by treating the local rate of change of soil water content as the sum of a plant-driven term, proportional to whole-plant transpiration, and a soil-driven redistribution term, and by assuming the latter varies slowly and linearly with time within a short measurement window. Under this assumption, a linear regression of the depletion rate against transpiration recovers the depth profile of plant-driven uptake directly from data, without requiring knowledge of the root hydraulic architecture.

This assumption has been validated by van Dusschoten et al. (2020) using synthetic data generated with a prescribed, non-compensating uptake profile. However, real root systems compensate: when local soil water becomes limiting, uptake shifts toward wetter regions of the root system through the hydraulic architecture itself (Couvreur et al., 2012). It is not established whether the SWaP regression continues to recover the true, root-density-based uptake distribution when this compensatory mechanism is genuinely active in the underlying data. The present work addresses this question directly, by generating the input soil water content time series with a mechanistic model that includes compensation by construction, and comparing the resulting SWaP-estimated Ûp(z) against the known true uptake distribution.

## 2. Theoretical Background

The macroscopic root water uptake model of Couvreur, Vanderborght and Javaux (2012) partitions the local sink term into a passive component, proportional to the Standard Uptake Fraction (SUF), and a compensatory component driven by the local deviation of soil water potential from its SUF-weighted equivalent:

$$S(z,t) = Up(z,t) + Us(z,t) = SUF(z)\cdot Tact(t) + SUF(z)\cdot K_{comp}\cdot(H_{sr}(z,t) - H_{s,eq}(t))$$

Coupled to the one-dimensional Richards equation (Couvreur et al., 2014a), the local water content balance becomes:

$$\frac{\partial \theta(z,t)}{\partial t} = Up(z,t) + Us(z,t) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\Big[K(\theta)\big(\tfrac{\partial h}{\partial z} - 1\big)\Big] \quad (+\ \text{evaporation at } z=0)$$

Because Us(z,t) cannot be separated from the pure soil capillary flux divergence using θ(z,t) alone, van Dusschoten et al. (2020) lump the two into a single redistribution term Sr(z,t), approximated as linear in time within each fitting window:

$$-\frac{d\theta(z,t)}{dt} = \hat{U}p(z)\cdot Tact(t) + Sr(z,t), \qquad Sr(z,t) \approx p_1(z) + p_2(z)\cdot t$$

The regression slope, Ûp(z), is by construction identical to SUF(z) whenever Sr(z,t) is genuinely separable from the Tact(t) signal within the fitting window:

$$\hat{U}p(z) \equiv \frac{Up(z,t)}{Tact(t)} \equiv SUF(z) \qquad \text{(exact, under the separability assumption)}$$

The instantaneous, ground-truth analogue of this ratio — available only when the true sink is known — is the relative sink:

$$RelSink(z,t) = \frac{S(z,t)}{Tact(t)} = SUF(z) + \frac{Us(z,t)}{Tact(t)}$$

The validity of the SWaP assumption is fundamentally a question of timescale separation between plant response and soil redistribution, governed by the soil hydraulic diffusivity `D(θ) = K(θ)/C(θ)` and the characteristic redistribution time `τ_soil ≈ L²/D(θ)`. The assumption holds where the fitting window `Δt` satisfies `Δt ≪ τ_soil`, and is expected to weaken as soil water content — and hence D(θ) — increases, or as the fitting window lengthens.

## 3. Materials and Methods

A one-dimensional Richards equation was solved numerically with the full Couvreur mechanistic sink term, using root hydraulic properties (SUF, Kr, kr, root radius, Krs) derived from measured root length density profiles. The soil column was forced with an alternating high/low transpiration boundary condition over seven simulated days, producing a progressive vertical drying gradient. The resulting soil water content time series, θ(z,t), was treated as if it were direct sensor output — replicating, in silico, the type of experiment performed by van Dusschoten et al. (2020) — and the SWaP regression procedure was applied independently within each of six ~2.2-hour cycles per day, at each depth.

Because the mechanistic solver provides the true sink S(z,t) at every time step, this configuration permits direct validation of the SWaP linearity assumption against ground truth, rather than relying solely on the diffusion-timescale argument used in the original methodological development. This constitutes a more demanding test than van Dusschoten et al.'s own in-silico validation, which imposed a purely passive, non-compensating uptake shape as the synthetic sink.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Depth-resolved behaviour

Figure 1 compares the depth profiles of RelSink(z,t), Ûp(z), and SUF(z) for a well-watered day (Day 1) and a substantially dried profile (Day 6). Under well-watered conditions, the three quantities collapse onto a single curve across all six diurnal cycles, indicating negligible compensatory activity. As the profile dries, RelSink(z,t) fans out markedly with time within each cycle, and Ûp(z) develops a secondary peak at intermediate depth that is absent from the fixed SUF(z) reference.

### 4.2 Structural basis for the depth-dependence

The passive uptake term is proportional to root density at each depth, `Up(z,t) = SUF(z)·Tact(t)`, so the absolute magnitude of Up at any depth is set entirely by the local value of the Standard Uptake Fraction. This proportionality has a direct consequence for the relative visibility of compensatory uptake across the profile: because Us is governed by local soil water potential heterogeneity rather than by root density, its magnitude relative to Up — and hence its capacity to alter the net local sink — depends critically on how large the passive baseline already is at that depth.

Near the surface, where SUF(z) is largest, Up constitutes a substantial baseline flux from the onset of drying. Early in the drydown, when vertical gradients in soil water potential remain modest, the resulting compensatory flux is small in absolute terms and therefore negligible relative to the already large passive term. Consequently, the near-surface sink remains dominated by Up, and the observed depletion rate provides a direct, largely undistorted estimate of plant-driven uptake.

At depth, where SUF(z) approaches zero, the passive baseline is itself vanishingly small. Under these conditions, even a modest compensatory flux is sufficient to become comparable to, or exceed, Up. However, because the vertical water potential gradient has not yet propagated to these depths during the early-to-intermediate stages of drying, Us remains small in absolute terms as well, and the net sink stays quiescent.

The intermediate depths occupy a structurally distinct regime: SUF(z) is sufficiently large to sustain a non-negligible passive baseline, while simultaneously falling within the range of the developing soil water potential gradient sufficient to drive a comparably large compensatory flux. It is this coincidence — a substantial Up baseline combined with a substantial, actively evolving Us — that produces the near-complete cancellation of the two terms observed at intermediate depths, and identifies this zone as the primary locus of compensatory activity independent of any direct measurement of the soil redistribution term.

This structural argument also accounts for the cycle-to-cycle evolution of the fitted regression parameters within a single day (Table 2): the fitted slope (Ûp) decreases at the top and increases at intermediate depth across successive cycles, while the intercept (Sr) shows the opposite trend at each depth. This reflects a genuine migration of compensatory activity from the surface toward mid-depth as the upper layers progressively deplete, captured by the regression as an apparent rise in Ûp at mid-depth despite the true SUF(z) remaining constant by definition.

**Table 2.** Within-day evolution of the fitted regression slope and intercept.

| Depth | Slope Ûp (Cycle 1→6) | Intercept Sr (Cycle 1→6) |
|---|---|---|
| Top | Decreasing | Increasing |
| Middle | Increasing | Decreasing |
| Bottom | Approximately stable | Approximately stable |

### 4.3 Time-resolved behaviour within a transpiration cycle

Figure 2 shows the regression scatter of −dθ/dt against Tact for Day 4 and Day 6, in which the fitted relationship departs visibly from a straight line, tracing a looped trajectory rather than retracing itself between the low and high transpiration states.

![Day 6 regression with more pronounced loop and degraded R-squared at depth](figures/day6_regression_10-40cm.png)
*Figure 2b. Day 6. The loop is more pronounced and R² degrades sharply at 30–40 cm.*

This behaviour corresponds to a three-phase evolution of the true sink within each cycle, established by comparing S(z,t), SUF(z) and the fit quality across the start, middle, and end of the high-transpiration period (Table 3).

**Table 3.** Within-cycle phase decomposition of sink behaviour.

| Phase | Sink relation | vs. SUF | Fit quality (R²) | RSWF |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T-start | S ≈ Up | S ≈ SUF | High | ≈ 0 |
| T-middle | S ≠ Up (transitional) | Deviates from SUF | Degraded | Non-zero |
| T-end | S ≈ Us | Settles near Us, not SUF | Recovers | ≈ 0 |

The regression is therefore reliable only near the two temporal boundaries of each cycle, where the sink has reached a quasi-steady relationship with transpiration. The middle of each cycle corresponds to the transient period during which compensatory uptake is actively catching up with the imposed change in transpiration demand, producing a non-linear, non-single-valued relationship between Tact and the local depletion rate — the direct manifestation of the breakdown of the linear-in-time redistribution assumption within a single fitting window. Formally, only the fraction of Sr(z,t) that co-varies with Tact(t) within the window biases the fitted slope; the remainder, varying smoothly with clock time, is correctly absorbed by the intercept term:

$$(\hat{U}p - SUF) \approx [\text{component of } Us + RSWF \text{ co-varying with } Tact(t) \text{ within the window}]$$

## 5. Discussion

Two largely independent axes of degradation of the SWaP assumption emerge from this analysis. Along the depth axis, reliability is highest near the surface, where the passive uptake baseline is large relative to the still-developing compensatory flux, and lowest at intermediate depth, where compensatory and passive fluxes become comparable in magnitude and nearly cancel in the observed signal. Along the temporal axis, reliability is highest at the start and end of each transpiration cycle, when the sink has reached a quasi-steady regime, and lowest during the transient mid-cycle period, when compensation is dynamically adjusting to the imposed forcing. These two axes compound: the combination of intermediate depth, mid-cycle timing, and advanced drydown consistently coincides with the largest divergence between Ûp(z) and SUF(z) and the lowest regression fit quality observed in the data.

To separate genuine compensatory signal from artefacts of the estimation procedure itself, we propose a three-term diagnostic, all components of which are available whenever ground truth is accessible (Table 4).

**Table 4.** Diagnostic decomposition of regression error.

| Comparison | Isolates |
|---|---|
| RelSink(z,t) − SUF(z) | True root compensation (Us) only — no estimation involved |
| Ûp(z) − SUF(z) | Total regression error (compensation leakage + soil-capillary leakage combined) |
| Ûp(z) − RelSink(z,t) | Soil-capillary (RSWF) leakage specifically — the compensation component cancels |

This decomposition indicates that a non-zero Ûp(z) − RelSink(z,t) even under low-compensation conditions — for example, early in the drydown — would signal that pure soil capillary dynamics, independent of any root-mediated compensation, are already sufficient to stress the linear-in-time assumption, a finding that would not be visible from a comparison against SUF(z) alone.

## 6. Validity of the SWaP Assumption: Summary

**Table 5.** Depth × time validity conditions.

| Condition | Ûp(z) ≈ SUF(z)? | Physical reason |
|---|---|---|
| Well-watered profile, any depth | Yes — near-exact | Us ≈ 0 everywhere; nothing to contaminate the fit. |
| Top layer, throughout drydown | Yes | RSWF ≈ 0; observed signal tracks true Up directly. |
| Bottom layer, early–mid drydown | Yes | SUF(z) itself small, so absolute error remains small. |
| Middle layer, once vertical gradient develops | No — systematic deviation | Us and RSWF become large and nearly cancel; true signal largely consumed by compensation. |
| Any depth, mid-cycle | No — transient failure | Sr is not linear in time within the window; loop artefact appears. |
| Any depth, cycle start/end | Yes, even under drydown | Sink has reached quasi-steady state; RSWF ≈ 0 at both bounds. |
| Severe, late drydown | No, distinct mechanism | S becomes dominated by Us rather than Up, independent of redistribution linearity. |

The SWaP assumption is therefore not adequately characterised as simply valid under wet conditions and invalid under dry conditions. It remains valid wherever the true sink is either genuinely dominated by the passive term — as at the surface throughout the drydown — or has reached a quasi-steady state, as at the beginning and end of each transpiration pulse. It fails specifically where compensatory uptake is both large in magnitude and actively evolving in step with transpiration, a condition that arises at intermediate depths, during the transient phase of each cycle, and that intensifies as the soil water potential profile becomes increasingly heterogeneous with time.

## 7. Conclusion

Using a mechanistic root water uptake simulation as ground truth, we show that the SWaP regression recovers the true root density distribution reliably only under a specific, identifiable combination of depth and cycle-phase conditions, rather than uniformly across the soil profile or the diurnal cycle. The intermediate-depth, mid-cycle regime constitutes the principal failure mode, driven by near-total, dynamically lagged compensatory uptake rather than by measurement noise or an arbitrary drift term. We recommend restricting Ûp(z) extraction to the start and end phases of each transpiration cycle, and reporting the three-term diagnostic proposed here alongside any future SWaP-based estimate, to distinguish genuine root compensation from soil-capillary estimation artefacts.
